# Human Voice Revision Protocol

## Purpose

Use this protocol when a user says a draft has "AI flavor," "AI率/AI味太高,"
"AIGC痕迹," "too polished," "too generic," "template-like," or asks for a
more student-authored, classroom, personal, or non-formulaic academic voice.

The goal is better writing: more concrete, more situated, more text-specific,
and closer to the user's own voice. Do not claim to bypass AI detectors or
guarantee a lower detector score.

## Preferred Workflow

1. If the user has a draft, revise the draft instead of regenerating it from
   scratch.
2. If the user can provide a prior low-AI-rate writing sample, ask for it and
   infer sentence rhythm, vocabulary level, paragraph length, and citation
   habits.
3. If no sample is available, infer a plausible voice from the assignment:
   coursework, thesis chapter, journal article, reflective essay, or literature
   review.
4. Preserve the requested academic structure, but make the prose less
   pipeline-shaped.

## Revision Moves

### Replace Template Abstraction With Local Evidence

Every analytical paragraph should open near the object being analyzed:

- literary paper: quote or paraphrase a specific line, image, scene, or word;
- social-science paper: name the actual finding, dataset, population, or
  institutional context;
- humanities paper: foreground close reading before broad theoretical claims.

Avoid paragraphs that begin with broad wrappers such as "From a cross-cultural
perspective," "This paper argues," "It is worth noting," or "In today's
context" unless the assignment explicitly requires a roadmap.

### Break the Smooth Pipeline

AI-like academic prose often has identical paragraph shapes. Vary the rhythm:

- allow one short paragraph after a dense close reading;
- let some paragraphs develop only one strong point instead of forcing three;
- use direct, plain sentences when the idea is simple;
- keep repeated technical terms stable instead of synonym-cycling.

### Keep Human-Sized Judgments

When the genre permits, especially coursework, literary appreciation, and
short essays, allow restrained first-person or reader-positioned phrasing:

- "I read this line less as emptiness than as quietness."
- "The detail that matters here is not the boat itself, but the lotus moving
  before the boat appears."
- "This comparison is limited, but it helps clarify..."

Do not add fake personal experience, fake fieldwork, or unverifiable memories.

### Reduce Uplift and Generic Global Claims

Flag and revise vague concluding phrases, especially in student papers:

- "shows important cross-cultural value"
- "has profound global significance"
- "responds to the common spiritual pursuit of humankind"
- "provides a new paradigm"
- "demonstrates timeless relevance"

Replace them with a narrower claim tied to the analysis:

- "The comparison makes Wang Wei's restraint easier to see."
- "The poem imagines nature as a place where human activity can become quieter,
  not absent."
- "The Western comparison is useful because it shows what Wang Wei chooses not
  to foreground: dialogue, biography, and explicit social conflict."

### Use Assignment-Appropriate Imperfection

Do not over-polish a coursework essay into journal prose. Keep:

- natural transitions rather than formal signposting everywhere;
- a few qualified judgments instead of totalizing claims;
- discipline-appropriate but not inflated vocabulary;
- concise citations instead of citation stuffing.

## Mini Checklist Before Final Output

- Does each body section contain at least one concrete textual or evidentiary
  anchor?
- Are there fewer generic meta-sentences than in the original?
- Do paragraph lengths vary for a reason?
- Are broad claims narrowed to what the evidence can support?
- Does the conclusion avoid empty uplift?
- If a user style sample was provided, does the revision preserve its sentence
  rhythm and vocabulary level?
